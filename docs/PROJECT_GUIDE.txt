1. THE ONE-PARAGRAPH SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Real networks (for example, who meets whom in a hospital) often have too many
connections to analyse or visualise, so we want to throw away most of the
connections and keep a small "backbone" that still behaves like the original.
A recent paper (Pereira, Costa and Rocha, 2026) argues that a family of methods
called "distance backbones" does this better than simpler methods such as
"keep the strongest connections". We re-implemented the paper's five methods and
tested its claims on two real contact networks. The paper's main claim about
SPREADING (how fast an infection travels) held on both networks. Its claim
about CENTRALITY (which people are most important) held only partly. We then ran
an extension to find out WHY the simple methods spread badly: most of the damage
turns out to come from the network falling apart into disconnected pieces, and
simply guaranteeing connectivity repairs most (not all) of the problem.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. THE PROBLEM: WHAT IS A NETWORK BACKBONE?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A weighted network is a set of nodes (people) joined by edges (contacts), where
each edge has a weight (how strong or frequent the contact is).

Dense networks are hard to study. "Sparsification" removes edges so that a
smaller network remains: the BACKBONE. A good backbone is much smaller but keeps
the properties we care about. The interesting question is: which properties?

Two kinds of fidelity (faithfulness to the original) are used in this project.
This split is OUR framing, not the paper's:

  Structural fidelity  = properties computed from the static graph itself
                         (who is central, is the graph still connected, are the
                         links between groups kept).
  Functional fidelity  = what happens when something actually runs on the
                         network (an epidemic spreading through it).

The central point of the project: a backbone can look good structurally and
still behave badly functionally, and vice versa.

Methods compared (the five from the paper's Table 2):

  W         Weight threshold. Keep the edges with the largest weights.
  DispFilt  Disparity filter. Keep edges that are statistically unusually strong
            for their endpoints.
  wEffRes   Weighted effective resistance. Keep edges that are important for the
            electrical-flow connectivity of the graph.
  SmD       Semi-metric distortion. Remove edges that are "shortcut-able": an
            edge is distorted if a path through other nodes is shorter than the
            edge itself.
  DBS       Distance Backbone Synthesis (the paper's proposal). Uses a one-
            parameter family of "distance backbones" and ranks every edge by the
            largest parameter value (lambda) at which it still survives.

Idea behind distance backbones: turn weights into distances (strong contact =
short distance). An edge belongs to the "metric backbone" if no indirect path is
shorter than the edge. Such edges are exactly the ones that matter for shortest
paths. The Dombi family generalises how a path length is measured:
  lambda = 1        -> ordinary sum of distances (metric backbone)
  lambda -> infinity -> longest edge on the path (ultrametric backbone)
  lambda -> 0       -> the whole graph
Backbones are nested: a larger lambda gives a smaller backbone.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. THE BASE PAPER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Title:    Distance backbones optimize spreading dynamics and centrality ranks in
          the sparsification of complex networks
Authors:  Pereira, Costa and Rocha (2026), arXiv:2603.14564v2
File:     papers/2603.14564v2.pdf

What the paper claims (as tested here):
  (a) DBS and the metric/semi-metric methods preserve SPREADING dynamics better
      than weight thresholding and the disparity filter.
  (b) DBS also preserves the RANKING of nodes by eigenvector centrality.
  (c) There is an optimal value of lambda (around 0.3) that balances fidelity
      and size.

The paper gives no code and no data link, so everything was rebuilt from the
text. Where the paper is silent, we made and documented our own choices
(Section 5).


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. THE DATA
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Both datasets come from SocioPatterns: people wore wireless badges that record
face-to-face contacts every 20 seconds.

  hospital    75 nodes, 1,139 edges   (file: data/detailed_list_of_contacts_Hospital.dat)
  workplace1  92 nodes,   755 edges   (file: data/tij_InVS.dat)

How a contact list becomes a weighted network (paper Eq. 1):
  r_ij   = number of 20-second intervals in which i and j were in contact
  r_ii   = total contact intervals of person i
  proximity  p_ij = r_ij / (r_ii + r_jj - r_ij)      (between 0 and 1)
  distance   d_ij = 1 / p_ij - 1

DATA CHECK against the paper's Table 1 (size of the metric backbone):

  Network      Ours     Paper    Status
  hospital     13.43%   13.43%   matches exactly -> verified replication
  workplace1   13.25%   20.63%   differs         -> NOT a verified replication

For workplace1 the node and edge counts match the paper but the metric backbone
is smaller (179 edges against the paper's 228). We tried many explanations
(other proximity formulas, duplicate rows, rounding, merging time steps, single
days) and none worked. The cause is unknown. For that reason workplace1 is
reported as a "second network" / robustness check, and ONLY hospital counts as
a verified replication.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. WHAT WE IMPLEMENTED (THE REPLICATION)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Everything is in the folder backbone/ and is driven by run.py.

Step 1 - Build the graph (backbone/data.py)
  Contact file -> proximity and distance for every edge.

Step 2 - Score every edge (backbone/backbones.py, sparsify.py)
  Semi-metric distortion, the metric and ultrametric backbones, and the DBS
  lambda for every edge (found by bisection). Also the scores of W, DispFilt
  and wEffRes. Every method is turned into an "importance" ranking: edges are
  removed from least to most important. Ties (many SmD edges tie) are broken
  with a seeded random order so results are reproducible.

Step 3 - Progressive sparsification (backbone/sweep.py)
  The adjusted size X runs from 0 (a spanning tree, the minimum that connects
  everyone) to 1 (the full graph):
      X = (edges kept - (n - 1)) / (total edges - (n - 1))
  For each method and each X we keep the top-ranked edges.

Step 4 - Measure fidelity (backbone/sweep.py, spread.py)
  See Section 6.

Step 5 - Summaries (backbone/summary.py)
  Area under each fidelity curve, and the optimal DBS lambda (paper Eq. 17,
  Table 3 weightings).

Choices the paper does not specify (our decisions, all documented):
  * Infection rate beta is calibrated so the full graph reaches half
    infection in about 20 steps (hospital beta = 1.51, workplace1 beta = 1.23).
  * Seeds: 15% of nodes, taken as top 5%, median 5% and bottom 5% by
    eigenvector centrality of the original network.
  * 100 SI runs per seed.
  * wEffRes uses proximity as conductance.
  * DispFilt uses the paper's formula with p = proximity.
  * A node never infected gets the maximum time (1000 steps).


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. HOW THE RESULTS ARE MEASURED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Three fidelity measures, each between the ORIGINAL network and the backbone:

  rho_C   Centrality fidelity (structural). Spearman rank correlation between
          the eigenvector centrality of every node in the original and in the
          backbone. 1 = same ranking of who is most important.

  rho_t   Infection-order fidelity (functional). Run an SI epidemic (below) on
          both networks. Spearman correlation of the average time at which each
          node gets infected. 1 = people are infected in the same order.

  xi^-1   Timescale fidelity (functional). Ratio of the half-infection time on
          the original to that on the backbone (median over runs). 1 = the
          epidemic takes just as long to reach half the nodes. Below 1 means
          the backbone slows the epidemic down.

SI model: one seed node starts infected. Each step, every infected node
infects each susceptible neighbour with probability beta * proximity.

AUC (area under the curve): each measure is plotted against X and the area from
a lower limit to X = 1 is reported, so one number summarises a whole curve.
The lower limit matters:
  * The paper's rule: each method is only scored from the size at which it
    stays connected (its own X-tilde).
  * Our "common limit": every method is scored from the same X, the size of the
    metric backbone. We consider this the fairer comparison, because the
    paper's rule rewards methods that fall apart early (they are only scored
    where they are already healthy). Both versions are reported.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. RESULTS OF THE REPLICATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

AUC with the same lower limit for all methods (higher is better; 1 is perfect):

              rho_C            rho_t            xi^-1
  Method      hosp    work1    hosp    work1    hosp    work1
  W           0.946   0.955    0.897   0.953    0.782   0.870
  DispFilt    0.954   0.964    0.781   0.881    0.672   0.777
  wEffRes     0.896   0.944    0.958   0.963    0.875   0.884
  SmD         0.920   0.964    0.971   0.971    0.870   0.896
  DBS         0.911   0.967    0.970   0.971    0.862   0.896

What this says:

  1. SPREADING: the paper's claim holds on both networks. DBS, SmD and wEffRes
     are clearly ahead of W and DispFilt on rho_t and xi^-1, and DBS and SmD are
     practically tied (the paper says this too).

  2. CENTRALITY: the paper's claim holds only partly. On hospital, W and
     DispFilt beat DBS (by 0.035 and 0.043). On workplace1 it is a near-tie.
     So "DBS preserves centrality best" is not confirmed.

  3. CONNECTIVITY: W and DispFilt lose connectivity much earlier than SmD and
     DBS. For hospital the size at which they stay connected is 0.474 (W) and
     0.800 (DispFilt) against 0.117 (SmD), 0.069 (wEffRes) and about 0
     (DBS). This is the clue that leads to the extension.

  4. OPTIMAL LAMBDA: hospital 0.346 (paper: about 0.3, agrees); workplace1
     0.607 (higher than the paper).

  5. The paper's own AUC rule makes W and DispFilt look best (hospital rho_C
     AUC 0.99 and 1.00) simply because they are only scored where they are
     still connected. This is why we also report the common-limit table.

Other replication checks:
  * Hospital's smallest and largest lambda match the paper's Table 2 exactly
    (0.133 and 16.276), which supports the DBS implementation.
  * The paper's log-normal fit of the lambda distribution is NOT reproduced
    (our spread is 0.6-0.7 against 0.387; the fitting recipe is unstated).
    These two checks were one-off and are not part of run.py.

SENSITIVITY (does the ranking depend on our choices?)
  * Changing the infection speed (half-time target 40 or 80) or using 300
    instead of 100 SI runs does not change the ranking of methods apart from
    ties of about 0.01 between DBS and SmD. AUCs move by at most 0.025.
  * An important finding: if DispFilt uses per-node normalised weights (the
    original Serrano formulation) instead of the paper's formula, it becomes
    much better on spreading (hospital rho_t 0.781 -> 0.919). The paper does
    not say which version it used, so "DispFilt is poor" is true for one
    version only.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. THE EXTENSION: WHY WE WENT BEYOND THE PAPER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A pure replication only checks whether the paper's numbers come out the same.
We wanted to add understanding. After the replication, three observations
raised a question:

  * W and DispFilt spread badly.
  * They also break the graph into several pieces early.
  * The centrality measure rho_C does not seem to notice this.

Diagnosis (backbone/diagnose.py, uses stored results, no new simulations):
  * Structure and function disagree in BOTH directions. For example DispFilt
    keeps rho_C >= 0.9 while rho_t < 0.8 at 40% of the sizes tested on
    hospital. The reverse also occurs: DBS has good spreading (rho_t 0.95)
    with poor rho_C (0.69) at hospital X = 0.2. So rho_C is a poor guide to
    spreading fidelity.
  * The number of connected components is strongly negatively related to
    rho_t (about -0.8) but does not hurt rho_C. Eigenvector centrality lives on
    the dense core of the graph, so it does not see lost connections.
  * Keeping many "bridge" edges (between communities) barely separates the
    methods at equal size.
  * Below the metric-backbone size every method degrades; at the bare
    spanning tree all are poor.

THE QUESTION OF THE EXTENSION:
  Why do distance backbones preserve spreading better than W and DispFilt, and
  what exactly do they add?

This is an explanation experiment, not a new method. We take W and DispFilt and
give them one distance-backbone property at a time, then see how much of the
gap to DBS closes. Three tests, using the same sizes, seeds, beta and reference
runs as the replication so all numbers are comparable:

  TEST 1 - Connectivity fix.
    M+T : first add a spanning tree built along the method's own ranking, then
          fill the budget with the method's top edges. Always connected.
    M+R : same but with a RANDOM spanning tree (control). If M+R does as well
          as M+T, then it is connectivity itself that matters, not which tree.

  TEST 2 - Shortest-path preservation.
    M+B : first add the whole metric backbone (it keeps every shortest path
          and is connected), then the method's top edges.
    M+X : the same number of RANDOM edges instead (control). If M+B beats M+X,
          being metric (not just being many edges) matters.

  TEST 3 - Bridge (between-community) edges, as a causal test.
    Keep the metric backbone, then fill the budget preferring between-community
    edges, within-community edges, or random edges. Communities come from
    Louvain partitions of the original graph (3 partitions). If bridges were
    the secret, preferring them should close the gap.

Headline number: the share of the gap to DBS that a variant closes,
      (AUC(variant) - AUC(start)) / (AUC(DBS) - AUC(start)).
Verdict rules were fixed BEFORE the runs, with a noise band of 0.02:
  "supported" if at least half of the gap is closed and the margin over the
  control exceeds 0.02; "partial" if the gain exceeds the noise band; otherwise
  "not supported".


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
9. RESULTS OF THE EXTENSION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Share of the xi^-1 gap to DBS closed (common limit = metric-backbone size):

  Test                          hosp W    hosp Disp   work1 W    work1 Disp
  1 Connectivity (M -> M+T)     36% part  41% part     5% no     40% part
  2 Shortest paths (M+T -> M+B) 12% no    14% no      34% no     42% part
  3 Bridges (M+B -> between)    negative  negative    undefined  negative
                                (not supported in all four cases)

Findings:

  F1. CONNECTIVITY EXPLAINS MOST OF THE INFECTION-ORDER FAILURE.
      On rho_t a plain spanning tree closes 77% (W) and 88% (DispFilt) of the
      gap to DBS on hospital, and 86% for DispFilt on workplace1. It costs no
      rho_C. A random tree does almost as well on rho_t, so for the order of
      infection it is connectivity itself that matters.

  F2. THE TIMESCALE IS ONLY PARTLY RECOVERED.
      On xi^-1 connectivity closes about 40% at most. The guided tree beats the
      random tree slightly (+0.03 to +0.06), so which tree matters a little,
      but the margins are about the size of the noise.

  F3. SHORTEST PATHS EXPLAIN PART OF THE REST.
      12-14% on hospital, 34-42% on workplace1; never half. Forcing metric
      edges beats forcing the same number of random edges (hospital W 0.817
      against 0.776), so being metric does help, but on hospital the added
      benefit on top of the spanning tree is small.

  F4. BRIDGES DO NOT EXPLAIN IT.
      No bridge-preferring fill beats the plain metric-backbone fill. The sign
      of any bridge effect even flips between networks (hospital: within-
      community fill is better; workplace1: between-community is better).
      Between-community fills also cost rho_C on hospital.

What remains unexplained: the rest of DBS's advantage comes from the way it
chooses the edges it adds (its lambda ranking), which we did not isolate.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
10. CONCLUSION, LIMITS, AND WHAT IS NOT CLAIMED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

CONCLUSION (on the two networks tested)
  The advantage of distance backbones over W and DispFilt splits into:
    (i)  a CONNECTIVITY part, which accounts for most of the infection-order
         advantage and is recovered by guaranteeing a spanning tree;
    (ii) a TIMESCALE part, which shortest-path preservation explains only in
         part and between-community edges do not explain.

CLAIMS SUMMARY
  C1  The paper's spreading result replicates; ranking is stable under beta,
      number of runs and tie-breaking.
  C2  The paper's centrality claim is mixed (fails on hospital, near-tie on
      workplace1). DispFilt's weakness depends on its weight normalisation.
  C3  rho_C does not predict spreading fidelity, in either direction, and
      cannot see fragmentation.
  C4  Fragmentation explains most of W and DispFilt's infection-order failure.
  C5  The timescale is only partly recovered by connectivity.
  C6  Preserving shortest paths explains part of the remaining timescale gap.
  C7  Between-community edges do not explain it.

PRACTICAL READING
  Judge a backbone on connectivity AND on a dynamical measure, not on rho_C
  alone. For weight thresholding and the disparity filter, a spanning-tree
  guarantee removes most of the infection-order penalty.

WHAT IS NOT CLAIMED
  * That the spanning-tree repair beats or replaces DBS or SmD (it does not:
    hospital xi^-1 is 0.811 against 0.870 for SmD).
  * That it is cheaper (not timed).
  * That results hold beyond contact networks.
  * Any statistical significance level.

LIMITS
  * Only two networks, and only hospital is a verified replication.
  * workplace1's data differ from the paper's for an unknown reason.
  * Only SI spreading and eigenvector centrality are tested (as in the paper).
  * The 50% / 0.02 verdict thresholds are our own choices; several outcomes sit
    just under the half line (34-42%).
  * No error bars on the AUCs; each lambda value has one stochastic run.
  * The lambda-distribution check and the school-network check were one-off
    and are not reproducible from run.py.

11. GLOSSARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Adjusted size X       Fraction of the "extra" edges kept, from 0 (spanning tree)
                      to 1 (full graph).
AUC                   Area under a fidelity-versus-X curve, normalised to [0,1].
Backbone              A small subset of edges that keeps the network's key
                      properties.
Bridge                An edge between two communities.
Connected             Every node can reach every other node.
Dombi family          The lambda-parameterised way of measuring path length.
Eigenvector           A node is important if it is linked to important nodes.
centrality
Fidelity              How faithfully the backbone reproduces the original.
Lambda (edge)         The largest lambda at which an edge is still in the
                      distance backbone; the DBS importance score.
Louvain               A standard algorithm that finds communities.
Metric backbone       Edges that lie on a shortest path (no shorter detour).
Proximity / distance  Edge strength in (0,1] and its inverse-based distance.
rho_C, rho_t, xi^-1   The three fidelity measures (Section 6).
SI model              Susceptible-Infected epidemic; infected nodes stay
                      infected.
Spanning tree         The minimal set of n-1 edges that connects all n nodes.
Spearman correlation  Correlation of ranks (1 = identical ordering).
Ultrametric backbone  Union of minimum spanning trees (the lambda -> infinity
                      limit).
X-tilde               Lower integration limit of the AUC (paper's own rule).

12. HOW TO RUN THE CODE AND WHERE THINGS ARE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Install and test:
    python -m pip install -r requirements.txt
    python -m pytest tests -q          (57 tests)

Run:
    python run.py                      full pipeline (26 min last run), writes results/ and ui/data.js
    python run.py --quick              10 SI runs per seed, writes results_quick/
    python run.py --networks hospital  one network only
    python run.py --force              recompute, ignoring cached results

Dashboard: open ui/index.html in a browser (data comes from ui/data.js, which
run.py generates). It shows the networks, curves, AUC, a live epidemic, the
lambda study, sensitivity, diagnosis, the extension and the conclusion.
Number keys 1-10 switch tabs. See the separate Dashboard Guide for each tab
and a demo script.

Folder map:
    run.py                single entry point
    backbone/data.py       contact files -> graphs, Table 1 values
    backbone/backbones.py  distortion, metric/ultrametric backbones, DBS lambda
    backbone/sparsify.py   the five methods and the connected variants
    backbone/sweep.py      rho_C and connectivity limits
    backbone/spread.py     SI model, rho_t, xi^-1, beta calibration
    backbone/summary.py    AUC and optimal lambda
    backbone/sensitivity.py sensitivity settings
    backbone/diagnose.py   diagnosis tables
    backbone/connected.py  the extension (three tests and verdicts)
    backbone/plots.py      figures
    backbone/export.py     results -> dashboard data
    tests/                 pytest suite
    data/                  the two raw contact files
    docs/RESULTS.md        full technical results write-up
    papers/                base paper and Correia et al. 2023
    results/               generated outputs (key tables and figures committed)

13. HONEST STATUS OF THE PROJECT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Done and verified:
  * Full pipeline runs; all 57 tests pass.
  * Hospital data reproduces the paper's Table 1 exactly.
  * The paper's spreading claim is reproduced on both networks.
  * Sensitivity, diagnosis and the three-test extension are complete.
  * A full re-run on 2026-10-21 (26 minutes) reproduced the same AUC tables
    and extension verdicts and regenerated the dashboard data for both
    networks.

Known gaps and suggested improvements:
  * A third network (the conference dataset, ht09) was planned but is not in
    the current code; adding it would strengthen the study.
  * Error bars on AUCs would make borderline verdicts more convincing.
  * The one-off checks (lambda fit, school networks) should be made
    reproducible or dropped from the claims.
  * Run time is now stated as about 30 minutes in the README, run.py and
    docs/RESULTS.md (the runs took 26 to 36 minutes, depending on load).
  * Work should be committed to git before submission.
